    The Vishwaguru Mirage: Why Geopolitics Won’t Save Us From Ourselves

    The world is tilting India’s way. A choking capital, a restless talent drain and a ballot box ruled by caste arithmetic suggest the contest that matters is at home.

    By ChaturOdysseus Published 9 November 2026 7 min read

    Every autumn, as the skies over North India turn into a leaden ceiling of toxic particulate matter, the national mood undergoes a peculiar cognitive dissonance. We light lamps, trade sweets, and celebrate the symbolic triumph of light over darkness while peering through hazardous smog on our way to pay inflated rents.

    The global stage appears to be aligning in India’s favour. Culturally, Nitesh Tiwari’s massive cinematic adaptation of the Ramayana aims to project civilisational lore across international screens. Geopolitically, the Western narrative of isolating rivals has collided with economic realism. Despite persistent lecturing on moral diplomacy, Western powers have quietly adapted to realpolitik, buying refined petroleum products derived from Russian crude processed in Indian refineries. External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar captured this Western ambivalence at the GLOBSEC Bratislava Forum in 2022:

    “Europe has to grow out of the mindset that Europe’s problems are the world’s problems, but the world’s problems are not Europe’s problems.” S. Jaishankar, External Affairs Minister, GLOBSEC Bratislava Forum

    Yet, while the tectonic plates of global influence creak in our direction, our internal civic fabric remains fraying, compromised by self-inflicted wounds, uncritical consumerism, and democratic negligence.

    Silicon Valley’s faltering halo

    For over two decades, the Indian middle class treated Silicon Valley as the ultimate high altar of civilisation. That pedestal is visibly cracking under its own weight.

    • Corporate vulnerability at the frontier. The myth of untouchable tech leadership has suffered an extraordinary blow. The New York Times reported that a woman accused of working for Chinese intelligence had sought close ties with Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang. He has since admitted an extramarital relationship with her, and Nvidia’s board has opened an independent inquiry while members of Congress have asked him to testify. Beijing denies any spying.
    • The cost of compute. The aggressive rollout of generative AI has created severe environmental strain. According to the Environmental and Energy Study Institute (EESI) and research from the University of California, Riverside, large data centres can consume up to 5 million gallons of water a day, much of it for evaporative cooling. That is comparable to the daily use of a town of 10,000 to 50,000 people. As tech conglomerates corner freshwater and grid capacity to power computational hype, the trade-off between basic human survival resources and server racks is becoming an ethical flashpoint.
    • The software dilemma. Tech executives themselves are sounding the alarm. Sridhar Vembu, co-founder of Zoho, has cautioned that the economic restructuring driven by generative tools is hitting white-collar stability, warning that any job performed on a computer now carries this risk and that adapting will be painful.

    Yet, faced with this looming disruption, the domestic tech ecosystem remains largely reactionary. Rather than pooling resources to build sovereign, foundational compute infrastructure and indigenous LLMs tailored to the subcontinent’s linguistic diversity, millions scramble for superficial certifications to preserve IT-services desk jobs. We remain digital sharecroppers on foreign infrastructure.

    The domestic contradiction: a nation on EMIs

    The starkest tragedy is not economic; it is behavioural. India remains caught in a loop of high-decibel cultural chauvinism paired with complete civic passivity.

    • The health toll. Data from The Lancet Planetary Health link approximately 1.67 million premature deaths a year in India to air pollution. During festive months the Air Quality Index routinely breaches 400 to 500, which the Central Pollution Control Board classes as ‘severe’. Yet air quality remains an afterthought in political manifestos, drowned out by film releases, social media controversies and sectarian mudslinging.
    • The expatriate paradox. We take immense pride in our ancient heritage, yet the primary ambition of our educated youth remains migrating to North America or Western Europe, often to jurisdictions whose political establishments openly undermine Indian sovereignty. We demand respect on the global stage that our own emigrating workforce trades away for foreign residency status.

    Complicity at the ballot box

    The most uncomfortable truth lies within our democratic choices. It is easy to accuse politicians of corruption, violence and institutional decay; it is harder to admit that they hold office by democratic mandate.

    According to the Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR), 46 per cent of the members elected to the 18th Lok Sabha, 251 of 543, declared criminal cases against themselves in their affidavits, with dozens facing serious charges including murder, kidnapping and crimes against women.

    We do not elect these candidates by accident:

    1. Identity over integrity. Voters routinely prioritise caste affiliation, local patronage networks and freebies over a candidate’s record on education, public healthcare and infrastructure.
    2. Indignation without ownership. After voting on narrow sectarian lines, the electorate spends the next five years lamenting systemic graft, poor law enforcement and bureaucratic apathy.
    3. The nature of protest. When citizens do take to the streets, collective action often deteriorates into vandalism, the destruction of public property and civic disorder, reflecting frustration rather than disciplined, policy-focused civic pressure.

    The real cleansing

    Geopolitical shifts are presenting India with an unprecedented historic opening. Western supply chains are diversifying away from Beijing, global energy routes are becoming multipolar, and international institutions can no longer ignore the Global South.

    However, international leverage is useless if built on a hollow domestic foundation. A nation cannot project power abroad while its capital chokes on its own air, its youth hesitate to build foundational industries, and its voters trade the ballot for caste arithmetic.

    If this festive season is to signify actual renewal rather than performative celebration, the inquiry must shift inward:

    • Demand clean air, functional urban drainage and uncontaminated water with the same ferocity reserved for social media outrage.
    • Refuse to vote for tainted candidates regardless of their caste or party flags.
    • Move beyond being the back-office labour force for foreign tech platforms and build sovereign capabilities at home.

    Until we demand standards from ourselves and our leaders, playing the victim on the world stage will not prevent China or other strategic competitors from seizing the century. Real power begins not in foreign capitals or global film festivals, but on our own streets and in our own voting booths.
